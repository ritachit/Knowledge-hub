Part I

The rails, the rents,
and who actually pays

How four-party card networks were assembled, why they earn more than the banks that built them, and where every paisa goes on a single transaction.

1.1 — Origins

A consortium beat a monopoly

The founding artifact is the Fresno Drop: in September 1958, Bank of America mailed roughly 60,000 unsolicited, pre-activated BankAmericards to residents of Fresno, California. It was a deliberate solution to the chicken-and-egg problem that kills every payment network — merchants won't accept a card no consumer holds, and consumers won't carry a card no merchant takes. BofA solved it by simply manufacturing one side of the market overnight.

It was also a financial disaster in the short run. Delinquency ran far above projections and losses in the first years were substantial. But the structural insight held: BofA had proven a general-purpose revolving credit card could work at scale, where earlier attempts (Diners Club 1950, charge-only and travel-focused; store cards, single-merchant) had not.

The decisive move came next, and it is the one most people skip. BofA could not expand nationally — the Bank Holding Company Act and state-level branching restrictions meant a California bank could not issue cards in Illinois. So in 1966 BofA began licensing BankAmericard to other banks. Simultaneously, a group of rival Californian banks formed the Interbank Card Association, which acquired the "Master Charge" brand from a Western States consortium.

Two consortia now existed. Neither was owned by a single bank; both were owned by their member banks. This is the whole story in miniature:

The structural insight

A single bank's card is limited by that bank's footprint. A consortium's card is limited only by how many banks join. Because members were competitors who all benefited from acceptance and none benefited from a rival owning the rails, the neutral-utility structure was the only one they would all fund. Network effects then did the rest.

Dee Hock, who ran the BankAmericard licensee programme, formalised this in 1970 by having BofA spin the system out into National BankAmericard Inc. — a members-owned entity. It was renamed Visa in 1976, chosen because it was pronounceable in most languages and needed no translation. Interbank renamed itself MasterCard in 1979.

Both spent the following two decades doing three unglamorous things: writing the operating rules that make interoperability possible, building the switching infrastructure (BASE I for authorisation in 1973, BASE II for clearing in 1974), and arbitrating disputes between members who were otherwise competitors. That's the moat. Not technology.

1.2 — Demutualisation

The banks sold the toll booth

Mastercard IPO'd in May 2006. Visa IPO'd in March 2008 — at the time the largest IPO in US history, raising roughly $17.9bn. In both cases the member banks converted their ownership into shares and, over time, sold down.

Why would banks give up ownership of the rails they built? Three reasons, in rough order of weight:

  1. Antitrust liability transfer. A bank-owned network setting interchange rates that banks receive looks exactly like a price-fixing cartel, because structurally it is one. Merchant litigation was mounting. Converting to an independent public company put a legal buffer between the banks and the rate-setting function.
  2. Capital. The IPOs monetised a stake most banks carried at near-zero book value. For banks reaching for capital in 2006–08, that was not a trivial consideration.
  3. Governance paralysis. A consortium of competitors is a bad vehicle for making fast investment decisions. Every member has a veto instinct.

The consequence — and this is the part worth internalising — is that incentives inverted. A bank-owned network optimises for member bank economics: it wants interchange high and network fees low, because members receive the former and pay the latter. An independent public network optimises for its own revenue: network assessment and data-processing fees. It is comparatively indifferent to interchange except insofar as interchange keeps issuers loyal.

Common error

"Visa and Mastercard charge merchants 2–3%." They do not. The networks set default interchange rates but the money goes to the issuing bank, not to them. The networks keep a much thinner assessment and processing fee. This distinction is the single most common misunderstanding in the industry and it changes who you should be negotiating with.

Metric trap #1 — volume is not comparable

Anyone comparing these directly without normalising is either careless or selling something.

1.3 — Structure

Four parties, or three

Everything downstream — pricing, acceptance, rewards, who can negotiate with whom — follows from this structural difference.

DimensionFour-party (Visa, Mastercard, RuPay)Three-party / closed loop (Amex, Discover, Diners)
Parties Cardholder · Issuer · Acquirer · Merchant. Network sits in the middle as a fifth, non-transacting party. Cardholder · Amex · Merchant. Amex is issuer, acquirer and network simultaneously.
Who sets merchant price Acquirer sets MDR; interchange (the bulk of it) is a network-published default the acquirer must pass to the issuer. Amex sets the merchant discount rate directly. No interchange exists — there is no second bank to pay.

The last row is worth pausing on. Amex knows that a specific cardholder shopped at a specific merchant, and it knows this for both the demand and supply side of the same transaction. Visa sees a transaction between an issuer it does not own and an acquirer it does not own. Amex's ability to run closed-loop merchant offers ("spend $X at this restaurant, get $Y back") is a direct product of the closed loop, and it is a genuine competitive asset rather than a marketing artefact.

1.4 — Amex

Spend-centric, not lend-centric

Amex predates all of them — founded 1850 as a freight forwarder, then money orders (1882), then travellers cheques (1891). The card came in 1958, the same year as the Fresno Drop, but as a charge card: pay in full monthly, no revolving balance, with an annual fee. That origin shaped everything.

A revolving credit card issuer makes money primarily on interest. It wants customers who carry balances. Amex's original model made money on merchant discount and annual fees. It wanted customers who spend a lot and pay in full. These produce entirely different businesses:

 Lend-centric issuerSpend-centric (Amex)

Ideal customerRevolver — carries a balance, pays interestTransactor — spends heavily, pays in full

The premium-spend flywheel is the actual product: high annual fee funds rich rewards → attracts affluent, high-spending cardholders → justifies a higher merchant discount rate → funds richer rewards. It only works if Amex cardholders genuinely spend more than the alternative, which they do. FY2025 billed business of $1,669.8bn across roughly 86.6m proprietary cards implies annual spend per proprietary card of roughly $19,000 — a multiple of typical general-purpose card spend.

The chronic weakness was acceptance. A merchant paying Amex's higher discount rate needs to believe Amex customers bring incremental spend, not just the same customers with a more expensive card. Many small merchants did not believe it. Amex's answer was OptBlue (launched 2014): let third-party acquirers sign small merchants onto Amex at rates they set, rather than Amex negotiating each one. Amex gave up per-merchant pricing control in exchange for coverage. As of mid-2025 Amex reports acceptance at roughly 160 million merchant locations globally, a large multiple of its 2017 footprint — though note that "locations" is a count that expanded partly through methodology and partly through OptBlue, not purely through new merchant persuasion.

The durable moat is corporate T&E. Amex's commercial business — corporate cards, expense integration, business travel — is genuinely hard to dislodge because it is embedded in finance workflows, not just in a wallet. Switching a company's corporate card programme means rebuilding expense reporting, and CFOs do not do that casually.

Where this is heading

The 2025 interchange settlement's end to "honor all cards" is, if it survives appeal, more consequential for Visa/Mastercard premium cards than it is for Amex — Amex merchants already chose to accept a higher-cost card knowingly. The irony is that a rule change won by merchants against V/MC pushes V/MC's premium tier toward the acceptance problem Amex spent forty years solving.

1.5 — Economics

What a network actually sells

If you asked a Visa executive what the company sells, "payment processing" would be a bad answer. The switching infrastructure is commoditised — NPCI runs a switch at enormous scale on a not-for-profit basis. What Visa and Mastercard actually sell is a bundle of five things, only one of which is technical:

1 · RULES
A written operating regulation that thousands of mutually competing banks in 200+ countries have contractually agreed to follow. This is the product. Interoperability is a legal artefact before it is a technical one.
2 · ARBITRATION
When an issuer and an acquirer disagree about a chargeback, someone must decide. The network is the court. Without a neutral arbiter, cross-bank disputes would require actual litigation and the system would collapse under its own transaction volume.
3 · BRAND / ACCEPTANCE GUARANTEE
The logo tells a cardholder in Lisbon that a card issued in Pune will work. That assurance is what allows an issuer to sell a card at all. It is worth more than the switching.
4 · SWITCHING
The actual message routing. Technically impressive at 65,000+ messages/second peak, but the least defensible layer.
5 · VALUE-ADDED SERVICES
Fraud scoring, tokenisation, consulting, data analytics, dispute tooling. The fastest-growing and strategically most important line — for Mastercard this reached $13.3bn in 2025, 40.6% of net revenue, growing 23% versus 12% for the core payment network.

Point 5 deserves emphasis because it signals where the networks think their future is. Core switching revenue grows roughly with global commerce — mid-single to low-double digits. Services grow at twice that rate and carry no interchange-regulation exposure. Every network strategy deck for the last five years has been, at bottom, a plan to reduce dependence on a fee stream that regulators keep attacking.

Why the margins are what they are

A Mastercard operating margin of 57.6% is not a pricing-power story alone. It is a fixed-cost story. The switch costs roughly the same to run whether it processes 150bn or 175bn transactions. Every incremental transaction is close to pure contribution. Combine that with:

  • No credit risk — the issuer eats defaults.
  • No customer acquisition cost at the consumer level — issuers spend that money.
  • Negative working capital — settlement flows through, it doesn't sit.
  • Two-sided network effects that have already resolved. There is no realistic new global four-party entrant.

The offset is client incentives — payments to banks to keep their portfolios on your network. Visa's client incentives were $15.8bn in FY25, up 14%, growing faster than net revenue. This is the competitive cost of a duopoly: Visa and Mastercard compete almost entirely by bidding for issuer portfolios, and issuers capture that surplus. Watch this line. If incentives grow persistently faster than revenue, the duopoly is more competitive than the margin suggests.

1.6 — Regulation

Twenty-one years of litigation

Interchange is the most litigated price in the global economy, for a reason that is genuinely hard: the merchant who pays it is not party to the contract that sets it. A merchant accepting Visa agrees to its acquirer's MDR, but the largest component of that MDR was set by Visa and is paid to a bank the merchant never chose.

InterventionYearWhat it didResult
Durbin Amendment (US)2010 Capped debit interchange for banks over $10bn assets; mandated dual-network routing on debit. Debit interchange fell sharply. Merchant-side implementation of surcharge and selective-decline rights realistically lags final approval by 12–24 months given POS and processor system updates.

The honest disagreement

Merchants argue interchange is a cartel price: set centrally, not competitively, borne by parties with no seat at the table, and inflated by a rewards arms race that regressively transfers from cash-paying (poorer) consumers to card-using (richer) ones via higher shelf prices.

Networks and issuers argue interchange is the price of a service — guaranteed payment, fraud absorption, credit extension, and a customer base that spends more on card than on cash. Cap it, and the EU outcome follows: rewards collapse, and there is limited evidence merchants passed the savings to consumers. The Durbin experience is the strongest empirical evidence for this position.

Both readings are supported by real evidence. Anyone who resolves this quickly is arguing a brief, not analysing.

2.1 — Mechanics

One ₹1,000 transaction, end to end

A customer taps a Visa credit card issued by HDFC Bank at a merchant whose payments are handled by Razorpay, which acquires through ICICI Bank. Here is every message and every party.

Phase A · Authorisation — target latency under 2 seconds
01
Card presented
Card chip → POS terminal

The EMV chip generates an ARQC (Authorisation Request Cryptogram) — a one-time cryptographic value derived from the transaction amount, a counter, and a key only the chip and issuer hold. This is what makes chip cards hard to clone: the magnetic stripe carried static data, the chip carries a value that is useless if replayed.

02
Merchant to acquirer
Terminal → Razorpay (PA) → ICICI (acquirer)

An ISO 8583 message assembles: PAN, amount, currency, merchant category code (MCC), terminal ID, acquirer BIN, the ARQC. The MCC matters more than it looks — it drives the interchange rate, the rewards category, and whether the transaction is even permitted.

03
Acquirer to network
ICICI → VisaNet

Visa reads the first 6–8 digits of the PAN — the BIN/IIN — to identify HDFC as the issuer, and routes accordingly. Visa applies its own risk scoring in flight and may attach a score the issuer can use.

04
Issuer decision
VisaNet → HDFC authorisation host

HDFC validates the ARQC, checks open-to-buy against the credit limit, runs velocity and fraud models, checks blocks. Returns approve or a decline code. This decision is where the auth rate is determined — the metric that matters most to every merchant and every PA, and the one they have least control over.

05
Response and hold
HDFC → VisaNet → ICICI → Razorpay → terminal

Approval returns with an authorisation code. HDFC places a hold on ₹1,000 of the customer's available credit. Critically: no money has moved. Authorisation is a promise, not a payment. The customer walks out with goods against a promise.

Phase B · Clearing & settlement — hours to days
06
Batch capture
Merchant → acquirer, typically end of day

The merchant submits the day's authorisations for capture. Capture amount can differ from auth amount — restaurants authorise the bill and capture bill-plus-tip; hotels authorise an estimate. This gap is a routine source of reconciliation pain.

07
Clearing
Acquirer → network → issuer

Visa computes the net position of every member. HDFC does not send ICICI one payment per transaction; Visa nets everything bilaterally and multilaterally and produces a single settlement figure per member per currency per cycle. This netting is a large part of why the network exists.

08
Settlement
Issuer settlement bank → network settlement bank → acquirer

Actual funds move, in central bank money, between settlement accounts. Interchange is deducted here — the issuer remits the transaction amount minus its interchange entitlement. The network never takes custody of the principal; it instructs.

09
Merchant payout
ICICI → Razorpay nodal/escrow account → merchant

In India, a Payment Aggregator must hold merchant funds in a designated escrow account with a scheduled commercial bank, per the RBI's March 2020 PA/PG guidelines. Standard settlement is T+1 for most merchants; the PA may offer faster settlement as a paid product, or slower for high-risk merchant categories.

10
Cardholder billing
HDFC → statement → payment

The transaction posts to the customer's statement. HDFC has funded ₹1,000 minus interchange and will not be repaid for up to 45 days. That funding cost is a real input to issuer economics and one reason interchange is defended as compensating for credit extension.

The float question

Between step 05 and step 09, money exists in a definitionally awkward state — committed but not transferred. Whoever holds funds during a settlement cycle earns interest on them. At scale this is material, which is exactly why the RBI's escrow rules for Indian PAs specify where merchant funds may sit and for how long. When you hear a PA discussing "float income," this is the mechanism, and regulators view it with suspicion because the money is not the PA's.

  • The PA's margin is roughly 0.2% gross — and out of that must come fraud losses, chargeback handling, engineering, support, and sales. This is why "we process $50bn in TPV" tells you almost nothing about whether a company makes money.

2.3 — Failure

What happens when it doesn't work

The happy path is the easy part. Operational cost, headcount, and most of a payments company's genuine differentiation live in the failure paths.

Declines

Distinguish two kinds, because the remedy is entirely different:

  • Hard decline — stolen card, closed account, invalid card. Retrying is pointless and repeated retries can be treated as abusive.
  • Soft decline — insufficient funds, issuer risk model triggered, temporary technical failure. Retrying with different timing or routing can succeed. Retry orchestration — deciding when and how to retry — is a real product, and it is the core of what an orchestration layer like Juspay sells.

A merchant seeing a 4% decline rate versus 7% is looking at a direct 3% revenue difference on card volume. This is why auth rate, not uptime, is the metric merchants actually negotiate on.

Refunds vs reversals vs chargebacks

RefundMerchantAfter settlement — a new transaction in reverseMerchant typically does not recover the original MDR. Refunding a ₹1,000 sale costs the merchant ₹1,020 in effect.

The chargeback ratio is a survival metric

Card networks impose monitoring programmes when a merchant's chargeback ratio exceeds thresholds — commonly cited around 0.9–1% of transactions, with escalating penalties and eventual termination of acceptance. A merchant losing card acceptance is usually a merchant losing its business. This is why risk teams have veto power over merchant onboarding at every serious PA, and why "we onboard merchants in 5 minutes" is a claim to interrogate rather than admire.

Who eats the loss

This is the question that drives every fraud-control decision in the industry, and the answer turns on liability shift:

  • Card-present, EMV chip used — issuer generally bears counterfeit fraud loss. The chip did its job.
  • Card-present, merchant used magstripe on a chip card — merchant bears it. This is the EMV liability shift (US: October 2015), and it was the mechanism that forced terminal upgrades without a mandate.
  • Card-not-present, no 3DS authentication — merchant generally bears it.
  • Card-not-present, 3DS authenticated — liability generally shifts to the issuer.

Read that last pair again: authentication does not primarily reduce fraud, it reallocates who pays for it. This is why merchants have complicated feelings about 3DS. It moves liability off them, but every additional step in checkout costs conversion. In India the RBI mandates additional factor authentication on most card-not-present transactions, so the trade-off is settled by regulation rather than by merchant preference — a genuinely important structural difference from the US.

2.4 — Tokens

Tokenisation and what it changed

A token is a surrogate value that replaces the actual card number. If a merchant's database is breached, the attacker gets tokens that are useless outside a specific context. Two distinct kinds, frequently conflated:

NETWORK TOKEN
Issued by Visa/Mastercard themselves through a Token Service Provider. Scoped to a merchant or channel. Because the network issues it, the network can update it when the underlying card is reissued — so a customer's subscription does not break when their card expires. This is a real merchant benefit, not just a security control. Visa reported over 16 billion tokens in circulation as of its FY25 commentary, against 10 billion in May 2024.
DEVICE TOKEN
What Apple Pay and Google Pay provision. Bound to a specific device with a device-specific cryptogram. The merchant never sees a PAN at all.
PSP / ACQUIRER TOKEN
A vault reference issued by a processor. Convenient but proprietary — it locks the merchant to that processor, since the token is meaningless elsewhere. This is a commercial lock-in mechanism dressed as a security feature, and merchants should treat it as such.

India took the strongest position globally here. The RBI's card-on-file tokenisation mandate, effective from 1 October 2022 after multiple deferrals, prohibits merchants and PAs from storing actual card numbers. Every saved card in India must be a token. The short-term effect was a measurable dip in card-on-file transaction success as the ecosystem migrated; the long-term effect is that India's card data footprint is materially smaller than that of most markets.

01 — The institution

A not-for-profit runs the national rails

There is no American or European equivalent to NPCI, and that absence is the point. The National Payments Corporation of India was incorporated in 2008 under Section 25 of the Companies Act — a not-for-profit — at the initiative of the RBI and the Indian Banks' Association. It is owned by banks. It operates as public infrastructure. It does not distribute profit.

NPCI runs, among others: UPI, IMPS, RuPay, AePS, NACH, Bharat BillPay, FASTag, and NCMC. In American terms: imagine if the Fed's payment rails, Visa, ACH, and the toll-tag system were one bank-owned nonprofit.

Before understanding UPI you need the baseline it replaced. India's pre-2010 retail payments were overwhelmingly cash, with cheque clearing that took days and a fragmented set of electronic rails:

NEFT2005Deferred net batchesRetail transfers, but batched — originally half-hourly, banking hours only.

IMPS deserves more credit than it gets. By 2010 India had a working 24×7 real-time retail settlement system. The US did not get one until RTP in 2017 and FedNow in 2023. What UPI added was not real-time settlement — that already existed — but addressing, authorisation and an open API.

The governance question

NPCI is a private not-for-profit with regulatory-scale power. It sets interchange, approves participants, decides which apps can access UPI and on what terms, and can throttle a company's business through a circular. Its board is dominated by banks, which compete with the fintechs it regulates.

The defence: a nonprofit had no incentive to extract rents, which is exactly why UPI could be free and why adoption was explosive. A for-profit network would have priced it, and India would look like Brazil at best.

The critique: a body with this much market power sits outside the accountability structures that apply to a statutory regulator. Bank-controlled governance over the rails that fintechs depend on is a structural conflict, whether or not it is currently exercised.

Both are correct. This tension has no resolution on the current institutional design, and anyone operating in Indian payments should treat NPCI circular risk as a first-order business risk.

02 — Architecture

Four addressable parties, one switch

UPI's design insight was to separate the identity of a payment endpoint from the bank account behind it. A Virtual Payment Address — ritachit@okhdfcbank — resolves through the NPCI switch to an underlying account. The payer never sees an account number, and the account can be switched without changing the address.

The second insight was to split the app from the bank. A PSP (Payment Service Provider) app like PhonePe or Google Pay is not a bank. It partners with a PSP bank that holds actual UPI membership. This is why Google Pay, with no banking licence, can run one of the world's largest payment apps — and also why a fintech's UPI business depends on a sponsor bank relationship it does not control.

UPI P2M — QR scan at a merchant◆ target < 5s end to end
HOP 1
Intent capture
Merchant QR → payer PSP app

The QR encodes the merchant's VPA, name, and optionally the amount. Static QR (printed, amount typed by payer) versus dynamic QR (generated per transaction, amount embedded) — the difference matters enormously for reconciliation, because a static QR gives the merchant no transaction reference.

HOP 2
Authorisation
Payer app → NPCI switch

The payer enters a 4- or 6-digit UPI PIN. Critically, the PIN is not validated by the app or by NPCI — it is encrypted at the device via the NPCI Common Library and validated by the remitter bank. The app never sees it. This is why a compromised UPI app cannot drain accounts on its own.

HOP 3
Resolution and debit
NPCI → remitter bank (payer's) → debit

NPCI resolves the VPA to an account, routes the debit request to the remitter bank, which checks balance, validates the PIN, and debits. Failures here — most commonly bank-side timeouts — are the dominant source of UPI failure, and they are attributed to the bank, not the app.

HOP 4
Credit
NPCI → beneficiary bank → merchant account

The beneficiary bank credits the merchant. The merchant's app shows confirmation, often via an audio speaker device — the sound box, which turns out to be one of the most commercially important products in Indian payments because it solves the merchant's trust problem without requiring them to look at a screen.

HOP 5
Settlement
NPCI net settlement → RBI accounts

Individual transactions credit in real time, but interbank settlement is netted in cycles through RBI accounts. Banks extend intraday credit against the netted position. This is the same architecture as card settlement — real-time to the user, netted underneath.

Two structural consequences worth naming:

  • The app has almost no control over success rate. Failure is usually bank-side. A PSP app can route across multiple PSP banks to improve success, and that routing intelligence is a genuine differentiator — but the app is fundamentally dependent on infrastructure it does not own.
  • There is no card number, so nothing to tokenise, steal, or leak. UPI's fraud surface is social engineering, not data breach. Which is exactly what the fraud statistics show: reported UPI fraud in FY2025-26 (to November) ran at roughly 10.6 lakh cases worth about ₹805 crore, up from ~6.3 lakh cases worth ₹485 crore in FY2024-25. Large in count, small relative to ₹314 lakh crore of throughput — and overwhelmingly a story of people being talked into authorising payments, not of systems being broken.

The 30% cap that keeps not happening

NPCI directed in November 2020 that no single third-party app provider should exceed 30% of UPI transaction volume, measured on a rolling three-month average, to limit concentration risk. A TPAP breaching the cap is required to stop onboarding new customers. The deadline has been extended three times: from 31 December 2022, to 31 December 2024, and — by a December 2024 circular — to 31 December 2026.

May 2026 data shows PhonePe at 46.2% and Google Pay at 32.7% — combined share dipping below 80% for the first time, with BHIM, Navi and super.money gaining. Both leaders remain far above 30%, and PhonePe would need to shed roughly a third of its volume to comply.

Enforcing the cap would mean degrading service for hundreds of millions of users to pre-empt a risk that has not yet materialised, which is why it keeps being deferred. But a dated deadline now exists and is close. This is the clearest live example of NPCI circular risk in the market: PhonePe's own CEO has publicly cited the cap as an overhang on its IPO timing, and no business plan that depends on UPI share should treat a fourth extension as given.

03 — The decision that shaped everything

Zero MDR

From 1 January 2020, following the Finance Act 2019 and CBDT notification under Section 269SU, merchants above a turnover threshold were required to offer UPI and RuPay debit, and no charge could be levied on those transactions — not on the customer, not on the merchant.

The most important sentence in this document

This is not a business model problem to be solved with better execution. It is the structure of the market.

When you evaluate any Indian payments company, the first question is not "how much volume do they process" — it is "what fraction of their volume is monetisable, and what are they selling that isn't payments?"

Who actually pays for UPI

The infrastructure is not free to run. Somebody funds it:

  • Government incentive scheme. Annual budgetary allocations to compensate banks for low-value P2M UPI and RuPay debit acceptance. The amounts have varied year to year and have consistently fallen short of what the industry says the cost is.
  • Banks, out of pocket. Banks absorb the switching, infrastructure and support cost. Their argument for tolerating it is deposit stickiness and data — UPI keeps the customer's primary account relationship live.
  • Apps, from adjacent products. PhonePe, Google Pay and Paytm run UPI at a loss and monetise through insurance distribution, lending referral, mutual funds, ads, and merchant services. UPI is customer acquisition, not a product.
Live policy debate — status as of mid-2026

Zero MDR on bank-account UPI P2M remains current law. But it is under active review. The Parliamentary Standing Committee on Finance tabled a report on 12 March 2026, and the Payments Council of India has proposed a tiered structure — reportedly around 30bps MDR applying only to large merchants, above a turnover threshold. No binding RBI or CBDT notification has followed.

The stakes are concrete. A business doing ₹100 crore GMV with a 65% UPI mix faces roughly ₹19 lakh of incremental annual cost per 30bps introduced. For the payments industry it would be the difference between a structurally unprofitable core product and a viable one.

For: zero MDR is an unfunded mandate that has made India's payments industry structurally lossmaking and dependent on inconsistent subsidy; large merchants can afford to pay for a service they benefit from. Against: zero MDR is precisely why adoption reached small merchants who would never have accepted a 2% fee; introducing MDR risks pushing the long tail back to cash and undoing the achievement. Watch this — it is the single biggest swing factor in Indian payments economics.

Demonetisation: separating the accelerant from the curve

The claim that demonetisation (8 November 2016) created UPI is common and wrong, though the opposite claim is also wrong. The honest reading:

  • UPI launched in April 2016, before demonetisation, and cleared just 373 transactions in its first month against 21 live banks. It was not ready.
  • Demonetisation's immediate beneficiaries were wallets, particularly Paytm, which had the consumer product ready at that moment. UPI did not yet.
  • What demonetisation genuinely did was create a national behavioural shock — merchants who had never accepted digital payment installed the means to, and consumers who had never used it tried it. That acceptance base was still there when UPI matured.
  • UPI's actual inflection came in 2018–19, driven by PhonePe and Google Pay's distribution spending, QR ubiquity, and zero cost — not by 2016.

Growth has been extraordinary and durable: from 21 banks and 373 transactions in the first month to 703 banks and 22.72 billion monthly transactions by June 2026. India now accounts for roughly 49% of global real-time payment volume — close to one in every two instant payments worldwide.

04 — RuPay

The domestic switch, and the credit card that isn't

RuPay launched in 2012. The rationale was explicit and had three parts:

  1. Cost. Every domestic Indian card transaction was being switched abroad and paying assessment fees to Visa or Mastercard in foreign currency. For a domestic-to-domestic transaction, that is pure leakage.
  2. Sovereignty. Domestic payment infrastructure that routes through foreign private networks is a strategic dependency. This argument gained enormous weight after the 2022 sanctions on Russia demonstrated that card networks can be switched off as an instrument of policy.
  3. Financial inclusion. RuPay debit was the default card issued under the Jan Dhan account programme, putting hundreds of millions of cards into circulation almost overnight. Volume by mandate.

RuPay is a four-party network structurally identical to Visa — issuers, acquirers, interchange, the lot. What makes it interesting is what NPCI did next.

RuPay credit card on UPI

Enabled from 2022, this lets a cardholder link a RuPay credit card to their UPI handle and pay by scanning a QR code. The user experience is indistinguishable from normal UPI. Underneath, the funding leg is a credit card transaction.

This is the most commercially significant construct in Indian payments, and the reason is not obvious:

Why this matters

India has roughly 400 million+ UPI users but only a fraction of that number holds a credit card. Card acceptance requires a POS terminal; UPI acceptance requires a printed QR code. Credit-on-UPI collapses that gap — it lets a credit card be spent at any of tens of millions of QR-accepting merchants who would never install a card terminal.

And critically, it carries MDR above ₹2,000. It is the only mass-scale construct that combines UPI's acceptance footprint with card economics. That is why every bank, every UPI app, and NPCI itself have pushed it hard.

Only RuPay is permitted on UPI. Visa and Mastercard are not. So every credit card issued as RuPay-on-UPI is a card that a global network does not switch and does not earn on. India is, deliberately, the hardest large market in the world for Visa and Mastercard to earn in. That is a policy achievement, not an accident.

05 — The regulatory perimeter

Licence risk is the business risk

In most markets, regulation is a compliance cost. In India, it is the primary determinant of who exists. Four interventions restructured the industry:

MARCH 2020
PA/PG Guidelines — the licence regime

RBI drew a bright line. A Payment Aggregator handles merchant funds and therefore requires authorisation, a minimum net worth (₹15 crore rising to ₹25 crore), and must hold merchant money in an escrow account with a scheduled commercial bank. A Payment Gateway only provides technology and never touches funds — no licence needed. Everyone who wanted to be a real business needed the PA licence.

2021–2023
The licence freeze

RBI stopped granting new PA licences while it processed the backlog, and barred unlicensed applicants from onboarding new merchants. Razorpay and Cashfree were both caught in this — able to serve existing merchants but frozen out of new acquisition for roughly a year. Competitors with licences in hand took share. This is the clearest demonstration in Indian fintech that regulatory status, not product quality, determines outcomes.

OCT 2022
Card-on-file tokenisation mandate

After multiple deferrals, merchants and PAs were prohibited from storing actual card numbers. Every saved card in India became a token. Short-term success rates dipped during migration; long-term, India's stored-card data footprint is far smaller than comparable markets.

APRIL 2018
Data localisation

All payment system data relating to Indian transactions to be stored only in India. Imposed significant re-architecture cost on global players and became an enforcement lever — Mastercard was barred from onboarding new Indian customers in 2021 over non-compliance.

JAN 2024
Paytm Payments Bank — the rupture

RBI directed Paytm Payments Bank to stop accepting fresh deposits and credit transactions after 29 February 2024, following persistent supervisory concerns and non-compliance. PPBL was where Paytm's UPI handles, wallet balances and much of its settlement infrastructure lived. Paytm had to migrate its UPI handles to four external partner banks and rebuild dependencies it had assumed were permanent.

The Paytm lesson, stated precisely

The failure was not a business failure. It was concentration of regulatory dependency: Paytm had built its consumer payments business on a single affiliated regulated entity, so a single supervisory action against that entity took down the whole stack at once.

The generalisable lesson for anyone operating in this market: enumerate every licence, sponsor bank, and NPCI approval your business depends on, and ask what happens if any one of them is withdrawn on 30 days' notice. If the answer for any single item is "we stop operating," that is your actual risk profile — not your churn rate.

The tension worth watching most closely

The friction axis is about to be repriced by regulation. RBI's Authentication Directions (issued 25 September 2025, effective 1 April 2026) require two distinct factors on domestic digital payments, with at least one dynamic for non-card-present transactions — and they explicitly permit passkeys, biometrics and device-bound credentials instead of SMS OTP.

That converts a CPO-versus-CRO argument into an engineering problem with a compliance deadline. It also creates a real competitive opening: whoever ships dynamic authentication that improves conversion rather than costing it gets a genuine, if temporary, advantage. Cross-border CNP requirements follow on 1 October 2026.

03 — Vocabulary

Terms that carry weight

Not a dictionary. These are the terms where using them precisely signals you know the business, and using them loosely signals you don't.

Structure

ISSUER
The bank that gave the customer the card and carries the credit risk. Takes the largest share of MDR via interchange.
ACQUIRER
The bank that holds the merchant relationship and settles funds to them. Must be a bank.
PA · PAYMENT AGGREGATOR
Handles merchant funds. Requires RBI authorisation, minimum net worth, and escrow with a scheduled commercial bank.
PG · PAYMENT GATEWAY
Technology only, never touches funds. No licence required. The distinction is regulatory, not marketing — people conflate these constantly.
ORCHESTRATOR
Sits above multiple PAs, routing each transaction to the best-performing one. Sells success rate. Juspay is the Indian example.
PSP / TPAP
In UPI: the app. A TPAP (third-party app provider) is a non-bank like PhonePe operating through a sponsor PSP bank.
PPI
Prepaid Payment Instrument — a wallet. Relevant because PPI-funded UPI above ₹2,000 carries 1.1% interchange while bank-funded UPI carries nil.
BIN / IIN
First 6–8 digits of the card number. Identifies the issuer and card product, and therefore determines the interchange rate.
MCC
Merchant Category Code. Drives interchange, rewards eligibility, and permissibility. Misclassified MCC is a common form of merchant fraud.
NODAL / ESCROW ACCOUNT
Where a PA must hold merchant funds in transit. A licence condition, and the thing an RBI inspection looks at first.

Money

MDR / TDR
Total fee the merchant pays, as a percentage. The pool everything else is carved from.
INTERCHANGE
The issuer's share of MDR — the largest component, typically ~70%. Set by the network as a default, not by the acquirer.
TAKE RATE
Net revenue ÷ volume. Always ask which volume. Take rate on total TPV and on card-only TPV are wildly different numbers in India.
TPV
Total Payment Volume. The most-quoted and least-informative number in the industry. Meaningless without the monetisable mix.
GROSS vs NET REVENUE
Gross includes interchange and network fees the company merely passes through. Net is what it keeps. The gap can be 5–10×.
CONTRIBUTION MARGIN
Net revenue minus directly variable cost. Definitions vary enormously — the number is only comparable if you know what was excluded.
FLOAT
Interest earned on funds held between collection and settlement. Real money at scale; regulators view it warily because the funds aren't yours.
TPAP FEE
Paid to consumer UPI apps for processing. Cut from 8bps to 6bps (non-industry) and 4bps to 3bps (industry) effective 1 April 2026.

Operations

AUTH RATE · PSR
Share of attempts that succeed. The metric that actually matters to merchants — a five-point improvement is worth ~25× a 0.2% MDR cut at the same volume.
TECHNICAL vs BUSINESS DECLINE
NPCI's distinction. Technical = system failure, addressable by routing. Business = insufficient funds or limits, not addressable. Conflating them flatters your numbers.
SOFT vs HARD DECLINE
Soft is retryable (insufficient funds, timeout). Hard is not (stolen, closed). Retrying hard declines is abuse.
CHARGEBACK RATIO
Disputes ÷ transactions. Networks impose monitoring above roughly 0.9–1%, with penalties and eventual loss of acceptance. A survival metric.
REPRESENTMENT
Contesting a chargeback with evidence. Manual, deadline-bound, and where dispute-ops headcount goes.
ARN
Acquirer Reference Number. The tracking ID for a transaction or refund. What support asks for when a customer says the refund never arrived.
SETTLEMENT CYCLE
T+1 standard in India; faster is sold as a product, slower is imposed on high-risk merchants.
E-MANDATE
Recurring payment authorisation. India requires AFA on setup and pre-debit notification — subscriptions are materially harder here than in the US.
STATIC vs DYNAMIC QR
Static: printed, payer types the amount, no transaction reference — a reconciliation problem. Dynamic: generated per transaction with amount embedded.
NETWORK TOKEN
Issued by the network, survives card reissuance — so subscriptions don't break on card expiry. Distinct from a PSP vault token, which locks you to that processor.

04 — North stars

Different players, different single number

Asking "what's the north star metric in payments" is the wrong question. It depends entirely on where you sit in the stack, and the answers are in genuine tension with each other.

PlayerNorth starWhy that oneWhat it distorts
Card network
Visa, Mastercard		 Processed transactions Fixed-cost switch; every incremental transaction is near-pure contribution. Volume in currency terms matters less than count. Encourages chasing low-value transaction count and bidding up client incentives to hold issuer portfolios.
Issuer
HDFC, Amex		 Spend per active card Interchange scales with spend, and the cost of servicing a card is broadly fixed. An inactive card is pure cost. Rewards arms race. Cards get issued to people who won't use them, inflating "cards in force."
PA / gateway
Razorpay, Cashfree		 Net revenue retention Merchant acquisition is expensive against a thin take rate. The business only works if merchants stay and grow. Nothing much — this is the honest metric. Which is precisely why it's quoted less often than TPV.
Orchestrator
Juspay		 Auth rate uplift It is literally the product. Measurable against a control, and directly convertible to merchant revenue. Attribution disputes — was the uplift routing, or a bank fixing its own infrastructure?
UPI app
PhonePe, GPay		 Monthly transacting users UPI itself earns nothing. The asset is a user with a payment habit who can be sold credit, insurance or funds. Transaction count becomes vanity — a ₹10 payment counts the same as ₹10,000. Drives the ₹-value-blind volume race.
Merchant Checkout conversion The only number that maps directly to their revenue. MDR is a cost line; conversion is the top line. Nothing. This is why the sophisticated merchants negotiate on success rate, not price.
The asymmetry worth internalising

At ₹1 crore monthly GMV, a 0.2 percentage point MDR reduction saves roughly ₹20,000 a month. A five-point improvement in payment success rate recovers roughly ₹5 lakh a month.

The success-rate lever is around 25× larger than the price lever — and most merchants negotiate exclusively on price, because MDR is on the invoice and failed transactions are not. This single asymmetry explains why orchestration exists as a category, and it's the most useful thing in this document for anyone actually buying payment services.

05 — Be brutal here

The numbers this industry hides behind

Each of these is technically true and functionally misleading. For each, what to ask instead.

01"We process $X billion in TPV"

The industry's signature number and its emptiest. In India, if 80% of that volume is UPI, then 80% of it generates zero revenue. A company processing $180bn of which four-fifths is UPI is not four times better than one processing $45bn that is all cards — it may be worse.

TPV also silently includes payouts, refunds and reversals in some disclosures. And it is trivially inflatable by onboarding a single large, low-margin enterprise merchant at near-zero take rate.

Ask instead

"What is net revenue, and what is your blended take rate on monetisable volume?" Then: "What percentage of TPV is UPI?" The second answer usually explains the first.

02Gross revenue quoted as revenue

A PA collecting 2% MDR passes ~1.4% to the issuer and ~0.2% to the network. It keeps roughly 0.2–0.4%. Reporting the full 2% as revenue overstates the business by 5–10×.

Accounting standards on gross-versus-net presentation give real latitude here depending on whether the company is deemed principal or agent, and companies choose the presentation that flatters. It is not fraud; it is a choice that most readers don't notice.

Ask instead

"Is this gross or net of interchange and scheme fees?" If gross, ask for net. If they resist, that is the answer.

03"Users" — which almost never means users

Registered users, app installs, MAU and transacting users differ by an order of magnitude. A UPI app with 400mn registrations might have 120mn monthly transacting users and far fewer who have ever bought a financial product.

"Merchants" has the same problem: onboarded merchants versus merchants who transacted this month versus merchants generating meaningful revenue. Long tails in Indian payments are extremely long.

Ask instead

"How many merchants transacted in the last 30 days, and what share of net revenue comes from the top 10?" Concentration is usually the more revealing number.

04UPI transaction counts

A ₹10 tea payment and a ₹50,000 transfer both count as one transaction. India's headline volume figures are genuinely enormous and genuinely real — but count-based metrics systematically overweight small-ticket P2P activity, which is the least monetisable segment of all.

The honest cut is P2M value, not total count. NPCI itself notes its published UPI totals exclude Credit Card on UPI and Credit Line on UPI, so the headline number is neither a complete count nor a revenue proxy.

Ask instead

"P2M value, not total count — and what's the average ticket size?" Ticket size tells you more about monetisability than volume ever will.

05Take rate on the wrong denominator

The most quietly effective distortion in the industry. Net revenue ÷ card volume is a respectable number. Net revenue ÷ total TPV including UPI is a much smaller one. Companies naturally choose the flattering denominator and rarely state which they used.

Ask instead

"Take rate on what base, exactly?" Then compute it yourself from net revenue and total volume, and compare to what they claimed.

06"Profitable" — with adjustments

Adjusted EBITDA excluding ESOP, excluding one-time restructuring, excluding impairment, at the segment level rather than consolidated. Each exclusion may be individually defensible. Together they can convert a substantial loss into a headline profit.

Razorpay is a clean illustration of why this needs care in both directions: it reported a ₹1,209 crore FY25 loss, but that figure is largely ESOP charges and roughly $150mn of tax from redomiciling to India — while its core online payments segment is genuinely EBITDA-profitable. The headline loss overstates operational weakness just as an adjusted profit would overstate strength. Neither single number is the answer.

Ask instead

"Show me consolidated GAAP net income, and separately the reconciliation from that to your adjusted figure." The bridge is where the story is.

07Contribution margin, defined creatively

Some companies exclude fraud losses. Some exclude support cost. Some exclude the sales commission that acquired the merchant. There is no standard definition, so the number is not comparable across companies even within the same market.

Ask instead

"What exactly is excluded, and where do fraud losses, chargeback handling and merchant support sit in your P&L?"

08Uptime instead of auth rate

The operational equivalent of TPV. A gateway can truthfully claim 99.99% uptime while a substantial share of transactions fail because a partner bank is degraded and traffic wasn't rerouted. Uptime measures whether your system responded, not whether the customer's payment succeeded.

Ask instead

"Auth rate by method, by bank, over the last 90 days including your worst day." The worst day is the useful number — averages hide exactly the events that cost merchants money.

09Success rate measured after the drop-off

Subtler and more common than it should be. If success rate is measured from transactions submitted to the bank, everything the customer abandoned at the checkout page is excluded from the denominator. The reported number improves while merchant revenue doesn't.

Ask instead

"Is the denominator checkout initiations or bank submissions?" Then ask for end-to-end conversion from checkout load to confirmed payment. That's the number the merchant actually feels.

10Valuation marks as facts

A private valuation is the price of the last marginal share sold under negotiated terms — often with liquidation preferences and ratchets that mean the headline number overstates common equity value. It is not a market price.

The 2025–26 Indian listings settled this empirically. Pine Labs listed at roughly $2.9bn against a $5bn private mark. Razorpay's DRHP reportedly targets $5–6bn against a $7.5bn Series F. The public market repriced both by roughly a third.

Ask instead

"What were the preference terms on the last round?" And treat any private mark from the 2021 cohort as an upper bound rather than a valuation.

06 — Practical

Questions that separate signal from pitch

If you have one meeting with an Indian payments company — as an investor, a partner, an acquirer, or a candidate — these are the questions whose answers you cannot get from a deck.

QuestionWhat a good answer looks likeWhat a bad answer reveals
What share of TPV is monetisable? An immediate number, with the UPI/card/other split, and a clear view on where mix is heading. Deflection to total TPV, or visible discomfort. They either don't track it or don't want to say it.
Which single external approval, if withdrawn, stops the business? A specific enumerated list — PA licence, named sponsor banks, NPCI approvals — with contingency for each. "We're fully compliant." That answers a different question. Paytm was compliant until it wasn't.
Auth rate on your worst day last quarter? A specific number, the cause, and what changed afterwards. Quoting an average, or quoting uptime. Averages conceal precisely the events that cost merchants money.
Top 10 merchants as a share of net revenue? A number, with a view on concentration risk and the take-rate profile of those accounts. Merchant count as the answer. Long tails are long and mostly worthless.
Is your non-payments revenue a real second engine? Segment-level revenue and margin, with the cross-sell rate from payments customers into it. Aspiration language. "Huge opportunity," "early days," no numbers.
What happens to your economics if UPI gets MDR? A quantified scenario. They've modelled 30bps on large merchants and know the revenue effect. Never considered it. It is the single largest swing factor in Indian payments and it is under live review.
How are you handling the 2FA directions? Shipped before 1 April 2026, with a conversion measurement, and a plan for cross-border CNP by 1 October 2026. Treating it as a compliance checkbox rather than a conversion opportunity. They've missed the strategic read.
Closing the loop

Three things carry across all three parts. One: the issuer takes roughly 70% of card economics, which is why everyone eventually wants to issue or lend. Two: India mandated its dominant payment rail to be free, which is why every Indian payments company is really a distribution business for something else. Three: volume metrics are the industry's preferred hiding place, and the discipline of asking "on what denominator, net of what" is most of what separates an informed read from a credulous one.
