The Float

How the oldest financial trade in the world turns other people's money into the most patient capital on earth.

float · noun

Money an insurer holds but does not own — collected as premiums today, owed as claims tomorrow. In the gap between the two, it is yours to invest.

— W. Buffett, Berkshire Hathaway Shareholder Letter, 2002

01 The Two Engines

An insurer is two businesses wearing one coat

Every insurance company on earth runs two engines at once. Almost everyone — including most people who own insurance stocks — only watches one of them.

The first engine is underwriting: the craft of selecting which risks to accept and pricing them so that, across thousands of policies, the premiums collected exceed the claims paid. The second engine is investing: taking the pile of cash that accumulates between collecting premiums and paying claims, and putting it to work. The genius of the business model — and the entire reason Warren Buffett spent six decades buying insurers — lives in the relationship between these two engines.

Here is the sequence that makes insurance unlike almost any other business. A customer pays you first. You provide the product — a promise to pay if something goes wrong — and you deliver on that promise later. Sometimes years later. Sometimes decades later. In the meantime, you are sitting on their money.

That middle band — the money in suspension — is the float. It appears on the balance sheet as a liability, because legally the insurer may have to pay it out. But functionally it behaves like an interest-free loan from policyholders that, in aggregate, never has to be fully repaid: as old claims are settled, new premiums replace the cash, so the pool stays roughly stable or grows. Section 3 is devoted entirely to it, because it is the single most important idea in the document.

The one-sentence version

An insurer collects money now, pays it out later, and keeps the investment returns on the gap. If it can also break even — or profit — on the underwriting itself, it has built a machine that is paid to borrow money.

02 Origins & Evolution

From a coffee house to a trillion-dollar idea

Insurance is older than the corporation, older than the stock exchange, older than modern banking. Its lessons were learned the hard way — usually after someone went bankrupt.

1688 · Lloyd's. In Edward Lloyd's London coffee house, ship owners and wealthy individuals met to share the risk of sea voyages. An owner would write the details of a voyage on a slip; backers would each write their name under the risk they would cover — literally under-writing it. This is the origin of both the word and the trade.

1693 · The first correlated-risk disaster. French privateers attacked an Anglo-Dutch merchant fleet, causing roughly £1 million in losses. The catastrophe wasn't that a few underwriters had backed the fleet — it was that a large part of the entire market had. Thirty-three underwriters went bankrupt at once.2 Parliament considered, and rejected, a bailout. The lesson — that risks which look independent can all fail together — is the same lesson AIG taught the world in 2008.

1956 · India nationalises life. Some 245 life insurers were merged into a single state entity — the Life Insurance Corporation of India (LIC). The general (non-life) sector followed in 1972, consolidated under the General Insurance Corporation (GIC). The goal: deepen penetration and stop mismanagement.3

1999 · India re-opens. Following the Malhotra Committee, the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI) was created and private players were let back in. HDFC Life, ICICI Prudential, SBI Life and others were born.

2022 · LIC goes public. The largest IPO in Indian history, and the sixth-largest globally that year — a state monopoly born in 1956, now answerable to public shareholders.3

Why the history matters

The structure of every insurance market today is downstream of these choices. India consolidated, then liberalised — which is why one state insurer still holds ~60% of the life market while nimble private players fight for the rest. The US never nationalised — which is why it has thousands of insurers regulated state-by-state. Same product, opposite institutional DNA.

03 The Engine Room Deep dive

The float, and the only number that matters: its cost

Float is not free money by default. Whether it is a gift or a tax depends entirely on one figure — the cost of float — and understanding it is the difference between admiring Buffett and understanding him.

Most people stop at "insurers get to invest other people's money." True, but incomplete, and the incompleteness is where fortunes are made and lost. The premiums an insurer collects usually do not cover the claims and expenses it eventually pays. That shortfall is an underwriting loss — and that loss is the cost of the float. It is the interest rate the insurer is effectively paying to hold policyholders' money.

The myth

"Insurance float is free money. That's Buffett's secret."

The reality

Float has a cost equal to the underwriting loss. For the P&C industry as a whole, that cost is usually positive — premiums have been inadequate to cover claims plus expenses for decades, and the industry's return on equity has trailed the S&P 500.4 Cost-free float is the exception. Buffett's edge is that his insurers achieve it; most cannot.

So there are three regimes, and they hinge on the combined ratio — claims plus expenses, divided by premiums earned:

  • Combined ratio > 100% — positive cost of float. The insurer loses money underwriting. The float carries a cost, like interest on a loan. It can still be profitable overall if investment income exceeds that underwriting loss.
  • Combined ratio = 100% — zero cost. Underwriting breaks even. The float is genuinely free. Every rupee of investment income on it is pure profit.
  • Combined ratio < 100% — negative cost of float. The insurer earns an underwriting profit and gets to invest the float on top. It is being paid to hold other people's money. This is the holy grail.5
Buffett, 2016 letter

This collect-now, pay-later model leaves P&C companies holding large sums — float — that will eventually go to others. Meanwhile, insurers get to invest it for their own benefit. Though individual policies come and go, the amount of float usually stays stable relative to premium volume.

Berkshire achieved a ~$9B underwriting gain in 2024 — meaning negative-cost float on ~$171B.1,5

Now make it concrete. The reason this matters isn't the float in any single year — it's what happens when you compound negative-cost float for decades. Larger float generates more investment income, which builds surplus, which supports writing more policies, which generates more float. A flywheel. The model below lets you turn the dials yourself.

04 Risk, Reserves & The Math Deep dive

The arithmetic of a promise

Underwriting looks like intuition and ends up as arithmetic. And buried inside that arithmetic is the single largest act of judgement on the entire balance sheet — one the insurer gets to make itself.

The foundation is the law of large numbers. You cannot predict whether one house will burn down. But across a hundred thousand similar houses, the fraction that burn each year is remarkably stable. Insurance converts individual uncertainty into collective near-certainty, charges a premium slightly above the expected loss, and pockets the difference. That difference, scaled, is underwriting profit.

The combined ratio, decomposed

The combined ratio is the report card. It splits into two parts, and knowing which part is moving tells you what kind of company you're looking at:

  • Loss ratio = claims paid ÷ premiums earned. The cost of the risk itself. A motor insurer running an 80% loss ratio pays ₹80 in claims for every ₹100 of premium.
  • Expense ratio = costs of acquiring and servicing policies ÷ premiums. Commissions, marketing, salaries, the agent's cut. This is where distribution (Section 7) shows up in the numbers — GEICO's direct model wins by crushing this ratio.
  • Combined ratio = loss ratio + expense ratio. Below 100, you profit on underwriting alone.

Two enemies sit underneath the loss ratio, and every underwriter fights them daily. Adverse selection: the people most eager to buy insurance are often the ones most likely to claim (the sick rush to buy health cover). Moral hazard: having insurance changes behaviour (an insured driver may drive less carefully). Price and policy design — deductibles, co-pays, exclusions — exist largely to fight these two forces.

Reserving: where management chooses its own earnings

Here is the part outsiders almost never see, and insiders obsess over. When an insurer earns a premium, it must immediately set aside a reserve — its best estimate of the claims that premium will eventually have to pay. But "eventually" can be years away, and the true number is unknowable at the time. So the reserve is an estimate. And estimates are choices.

The largest and most subjective piece is IBNR — Incurred But Not Reported. These are claims for events that have already happened but that nobody has filed yet. A car crash on December 31st whose claim arrives in March; an asbestos exposure in 1975 whose cancer surfaces in 2010. The actuary must reserve for losses that exist but are invisible. The tools — the chain-ladder method, the Bornhuetter-Ferguson method — project ultimate losses from how past claims "developed" over time in a triangle of accident-year-by-payment-year data.

The lever nobody talks about

Because reserves are estimates, they are the most powerful legal earnings-management tool in the industry. Over-reserve in a good year (hiding profit, building a cushion), then release reserves in a weak year (when prior-year claims come in below estimate, the surplus flows straight to current earnings). Analysts scrutinise "prior-year reserve development" precisely because a company can flatter today's results by quietly releasing yesterday's conservatism. Strong, consistent reserve releases can signal prudence — or a company running out of road.

This is why Buffett harps on reserve discipline and why he had to fix General Re after acquiring it in 1998 — its reserving had grown loose (Section 6). An insurer that under-reserves looks brilliantly profitable right up until the claims actually arrive and bankrupt it. The graveyard is full of them (Section 12).

05 The Shape of Time

Why some premiums stay investable for 40 years

Not all float is created equal. The single variable that decides how valuable a line of insurance is to a float-investor is how long the money sits before it leaves.

An insurer's lines are described by their tail — the lag between collecting the premium and paying the claim. Short-tail lines settle fast: a motor crash or a stolen phone is reported and paid within months. Long-tail lines settle slowly: a workers' compensation injury, a professional-liability suit, or asbestos exposure can pay out over decades. Buffett explicitly prized this — in extreme cases like asbestos, "payments can stretch over many decades," which means the float from those policies is investable for that entire span.1

The trade-off: long-tail float is more valuable (you hold it longer) but also more dangerous (you must reserve accurately for claims you won't truly understand for years — see reserving). The slider below shows roughly how long ₹100 of premium remains in the float across different lines.

06 The Builder

How one man turned float into Berkshire

Buffett didn't invent float. He understood it more clearly than anyone alive, then spent fifty-seven years assembling the machine that exploits it best.

The sequence is worth knowing because each acquisition added a different kind of float:

  • 1967 · National Indemnity. Buffett's first insurer, bought for Berkshire. A specialist in unusual risks others wouldn't price. This was the seed.5
  • GEICO. The direct-to-consumer auto insurer with a structural cost advantage — no agents, so a permanently lower expense ratio. Buffett began buying in and eventually owned it whole. Distribution as moat (Section 7).
  • 1998 · General Re. A major global reinsurer that hugely expanded the float pool. But it came with undisciplined underwriting and reserving that Buffett had to spend years correcting. Once fixed, it became a powerful contributor. The cautionary half of the story.5
  • Berkshire Hathaway Reinsurance Group (BHRG). Buffett's in-house engine for writing the largest, strangest, most complex risks on earth — catastrophe covers and retroactive loss-portfolio transfers with limits above $10B that no one else will touch. Enormous, long-tail, high-value float.5
Buffett's Alpha · the academic verdict

A landmark paper by Frazzini, Kabiller and Pedersen found Buffett's returns were "neither luck nor magic" but a reward for buying safe, high-quality stocks and applying leverage of about 1.6-to-1. That leverage came largely from insurance float. Their blunt conclusion: without the leverage, his returns would be nowhere near as good.6

Stock-picking mattered. But float was the force multiplier.

The deepest insight is structural. Most insurers invest their float almost entirely in bonds, because regulators want the money safe and liquid. Berkshire was allowed to invest the bulk in equities — and even to buy whole companies. Why the latitude? Because even if the entire float were wiped out tomorrow, Berkshire still owns a giant railroad, a giant utility, and dozens of other businesses. Regulators care that liabilities can be met; Berkshire's non-insurance empire is the buffer that lets it take more risk with the float than any ordinary insurer ever could.7 The float advantage compounds the equity advantage. That is the whole trick, and it is nearly impossible to copy.

07 Who Owns the Customer Deep dive

Distribution is the business; the product is a commodity

Anyone can design an insurance policy. The hard part — the part that builds durable moats and decides who wins — is getting it into customers' hands cheaply. In insurance, whoever controls distribution controls the economics.

An insurance policy is one of the most commoditised products in finance. Two motor policies, two term-life plans, are nearly identical. So competition doesn't happen mainly on the product — it happens on distribution, and distribution shows up directly in the expense ratio (Section 4). There are four main channels, and the choice between them defines the company:

  • Agency. Individual agents sell on commission. High-touch, high-cost, deep in rural and relationship-driven markets. LIC's army of agents is the largest such force in the world and remains its core moat.
  • Bancassurance. Banks sell insurance to their own account holders. This is the decisive channel in India — and it explains the entire competitive structure of Indian private life insurance. SBI Life (State Bank of India), HDFC Life (HDFC Bank) and ICICI Prudential (ICICI Bank) dominate precisely because they were born attached to the country's largest bank branch networks. The bank is the moat.
  • Direct. No intermediary — the customer buys online or by phone. Lowest cost, structurally lower expense ratio. GEICO built its entire US advantage on this; it is also the model behind digital-first insurers like Lemonade and Acko.
  • Broker. Independent brokers represent the customer across multiple insurers — dominant in commercial and corporate lines, and in US large-account business.
The insider's lens

When you evaluate an insurer, ask first: who owns its distribution? A life insurer with a captive bancassurance pipeline (SBI Life) has a fundamentally cheaper, more durable customer-acquisition engine than one buying business through open-market agents. A motor insurer going direct (GEICO) can underprice the agent-laden incumbents and still profit. Distribution explains more about long-run insurance returns than product design ever will — and it is why the Zurich–Kotak and Bajaj–Allianz deals were really fights over access to customers, not over underwriting talent.

This is also why "InsurTech" disruption has been harder than promised. A clever app can improve the buying experience, but it still has to solve the brutal economics of acquiring each customer for less than that customer is worth — and incumbents with bank branches or agent armies already have that solved. Distribution is the wall most disruptors break against.

Three differences matter most for anyone trying to understand — not just admire — these markets:

  • Maturity & headroom. India's low penetration is not a weakness — it is the entire bull case. McKinsey describes the country as sitting at the foot of an insurance "S-curve," with rapid growth ahead as incomes rise.10 The US is a saturated, slow-growth, share-stealing market. Indian premiums are projected to grow fastest among G20 nations.9
  • Regulatory architecture. India has one regulator (IRDAI) setting national rules — recently raising FDI to 100%, launching the Bima Sugam digital marketplace, and targeting "Insurance for All by 2047."8 The US has fifty state regulators coordinated loosely through the NAIC, so an insurer operating nationally navigates fifty rulebooks.
  • How the market prices them. This is the subtle one. Indian life insurers trade at a premium of seven to ten times book value — because the market is paying for decades of future growth in embedded value (Section 10).10 US P&C insurers typically trade near one to two times book, valued on current returns. Applying a US P&C mental model to an Indian life insurer (or vice versa) will give you a wildly wrong answer. They are different instruments.
The bridge an analyst must build

You cannot compare an Indian insurer's reported profit to a US insurer's directly. India reports on IRDAI statutory / Ind-AS bases; US insurers on US GAAP; and from 2023 the global IFRS-17 standard rewrote how insurers everywhere recognise profit (Section 9). Add embedded-value accounting for life insurers on top. "Profit" means a different thing in each frame. The competent analyst always asks which accounting basis before comparing two numbers.

09 Rules, Capital & Accounting

The constraints that decide how the float can be used

Regulation isn't a footnote to the float story — it is the cage the float lives in. The rules on capital and accounting determine how aggressively any insurer can invest, and how its profits even get counted.

The regulator's core job is solvency: making sure the insurer can pay claims even after a bad year. The main instrument is a capital requirement — the insurer must hold a buffer of capital above its liabilities. In Europe this is Solvency II; India is moving toward a risk-based capital (RBC) regime; the US uses RBC standards coordinated by the NAIC. The more risk you take with the float, the more capital you must hold against it. This is the real reason most insurers hold bonds, not equities — equities demand far more regulatory capital. Berkshire's latitude to hold equities (Section 6) is the exception that proves the rule.

IFRS-17: the quiet earthquake (2023)

In 2023, a new global accounting standard — IFRS-17 — came into force, rewriting how insurers worldwide recognise profit from contracts. Instead of booking premium as revenue when received, insurers now release profit as they deliver service over the life of the contract (via a "contractual service margin"). The practical effect: reported profits for the same underlying business can look completely different pre- and post-IFRS-17, and cross-border comparisons got harder, not easier. Any comparison spanning 2023 must account for it.

Statutory vs GAAP vs EV

An insurer can report three different "profits" for the same year. Statutory accounting (what regulators see) is deliberately conservative — built to test solvency. GAAP / IFRS (what investors see) aims to match revenue and cost economically. Embedded value (life insurers) adds the present value of future profits already locked into in-force policies. The classic illustration: an insurer's IFRS profit and its embedded-value profit can differ by roughly 2× in the same year.11 None is "the truth" — each answers a different question.

Health insurance — a third animal

Life and P&C get the attention, but in India health insurance is now the largest non-life segment — about 41% of non-life premium in FY25, overtaking motor, driven by rising medical costs and post-pandemic awareness.8 It behaves like neither life nor traditional P&C. Its defining dynamics: the medical loss ratio (share of premium paid out as medical claims), the economics of cashless networks and third-party administrators, and a relentless claims-inflation treadmill as healthcare costs outrun premium increases. A health insurer that misprices medical inflation can run a great-looking book straight into a wall.

10 Putting a Price on It Deep dive

How to value an insurer (and why P/E lies)

Valuing an insurer with the tools you'd use for a normal company will mislead you. The cash flows are muddied, the earnings are reserve-dependent, and the right lens depends on whether you're looking at life or non-life.

Why does the standard P/E ratio fail for insurers? Two reasons. First, a large share of reported profit comes from contracts signed in prior years — an insurer could write zero new policies this year and still report a profit, so this year's earnings say little about the franchise's health.12 Second, earnings are hostage to reserve estimates (Section 4), which management can flex. And DCF struggles too: the investment portfolio's cash flows swamp the operating cash flows, so it's hard to isolate what the insurance business itself is generating.13

So professionals reach for a different toolkit:

  • Price-to-Book (P/B) + Return on Equity (ROE). The workhorse pair. Because an insurer's assets are mostly marketable securities, book value is reasonably "real." The key relationship: a higher sustainable ROE justifies a higher P/B. An ROE around 10% covers the cost of capital; mid-teens is a well-run insurer.13 You buy a high-ROE insurer at a reasonable P/B — and avoid paying a high P/B for a low-ROE one.
  • Combined ratio. The qualitative gate for P&C. No durable underwriting profit, no premium multiple.
  • Embedded Value (EV) & Value of In-Force (VIF). The standard for life insurers. EV = net asset value + the present value of future profits from policies already written. Life insurers are then valued on a Price / EV multiple. A P/EV above 1 means the market expects future new business to add value beyond the existing book — which is exactly the bet embedded in those 7–10× P/B Indian life valuations.11
  • Other Comprehensive Income (OCI). Where unrealised investment gains/losses hide. Ignoring it means missing what the float portfolio is actually doing to book value.13

11 The Consumer's Side

How insurers fool you — and why the incentives demand it

Once you understand float, the customer-facing tricks stop looking like isolated bad behaviour and start looking like exactly what the business model rewards. The incentives don't excuse the tactics — they explain them.

The most important consumer-protection idea in all of insurance: mixing insurance with investment is almost always a bad deal for you and a great deal for the seller. The flagship offender in India is the ULIP — the Unit-Linked Insurance Plan.

The sentence that traps people

"Sir, this is like an FD, only better." Months later the document reveals the truth: ULIP, surrender charge, non-guaranteed bonus, five-year lock-in.14 The pitch sells certainty; the contract delivers the opposite.

Why is the ULIP mis-sold so relentlessly? Follow the money — the charges are enormous and front-loaded:

  • Premium allocation charges of 20–40% in year one — meaning a large slice of your first-year premium never reaches the investment at all.15
  • Fund management fees (~1.35%/yr), policy administration charges, and mortality charges that rise with age, layered on top.15
  • A five-year lock-in with surrender penalties — versus a mutual fund you can exit any day.15
  • High agent commissions — the structural reason the product is pushed so hard. The incentive to sell is baked into the charge structure.

The defences, worth knowing: the free-look period (cancel within ~15–30 days of receiving the policy for a near-full refund); keeping written proof of any false promise (WhatsApp, email, brochure, recordings); and escalation through the insurer's grievance cell, then Bima Bharosa, then the Insurance Ombudsman.14 And: ignore any "IRDAI refund" call asking for a fee or OTP — those are scams.

The claims playbook: delay, deny, defend

On the claims side, the float model creates a perverse incentive that is structural, not incidental. Every day an insurer delays paying your claim is another day it earns investment income on money that is, by then, owed to you. Testimony before US legislators put the scale starkly: a very large insurer delaying claim payments by even a single day could earn investment income reaching into the millions.16 Multiply across an entire book and "slow claims" stop being an operational failure and start looking like a profit centre. Common tactics documented across markets: requesting endless documentation, undervaluing damage, leaning on technical non-disclosure to repudiate, and simply being slow.16,17

Marketing gimmick

"98% claim settlement ratio!" — quoted prominently in every life-insurance ad.

What to actually check

Settlement ratio by count can hide rejections of large or complex claims, and says nothing about speed or partial settlements. For health and motor, repudiation tends to cluster in exactly the high-value claims you'd most want paid. Read the ratio — but read the exclusions, the speed, and the amount-weighted record harder.18

None of this means insurance is a scam — it is one of the most useful financial inventions in history, and genuine protection (term life, real health cover, liability) is worth buying. It means you should buy insurance for protection and keep your investing separate, read the contract rather than the pitch, and treat any product blending the two with deep suspicion.

12 The Graveyard

When the float turns on you

Buffett says it himself: leverage is leverage. Float is borrowed money, and borrowed money cuts both ways. The history of insurance is also a history of spectacular collapse.

Float magnifies a good underwriter and destroys a bad one. Three failure modes recur, and each has a famous corpse:

  • The long-tail reserve time-bomb. The very long-tail lines that make float so valuable (Section 5) are the ones most easily under-reserved. Asbestos and environmental liability bankrupted insurers decades after they wrote the policies — claims surfaced in the 1980s–2000s for exposures dating to mid-century. The premiums were long spent; the float assumption that "we'll have the money when claims come" failed because the claims were far larger and later than anyone reserved for.
  • Investing the float badly — the AIG lesson. In 2008, AIG nearly took down the global financial system. The fatal wound wasn't its core insurance underwriting — it was AIG Financial Products, a unit that wrote credit-default swaps and effectively bet the company's balance-sheet strength on the mortgage market. The float-backed empire was sound; the speculation attached to it was not. Lesson: how you deploy the float can kill you even when the insurance is fine.
  • Correlated catastrophe — the 1693 lesson, forever. When risks that looked independent all fail together, the law of large numbers breaks (Section 2). A single mega-catastrophe, or a cluster, can wipe out years of accumulated float at once. This is why reinsurance and catastrophe modelling exist (Section 13) — and why climate change is the live threat to the whole model.
The discipline that prevents it

Buffett's relentless emphasis on underwriting discipline and honest reserving isn't folksy conservatism — it's the only known defence against these three failure modes. He has repeatedly walked away from premium volume when prices were inadequate, accepting a smaller float rather than a mispriced one. The insurers in the graveyard chased the volume. That is the entire difference.

13 What the Top 0.001% Know

The deep structure

Beyond float and combined ratios lies the machinery that actually moves global risk. These are the ideas that separate someone who understands an insurer from someone who understands the insurance system.

① Reinsurance & retrocession

Insurers insure people; reinsurers insure insurers. When a primary insurer doesn't want to hold all of a risk, it cedes part of the premium (and risk) to a reinsurer like Munich Re, Swiss Re, GIC Re (India's dominant reinsurer, >50% of ceded premium18), or Berkshire's BHRG. Reinsurers themselves offload tail risk to other reinsurers — that's retrocession. The result is a global, layered web that spreads a Florida hurricane's losses across balance sheets on three continents. India is now building GIFT City as a global reinsurance hub to capture more of this flow domestically.8

② Catastrophe bonds & ILS

When risks are too large even for reinsurance, they get sold to capital markets as catastrophe bonds — instruments where investors earn a high yield but lose principal if a defined catastrophe (a quake of magnitude X in region Y) occurs. This is Insurance-Linked Securities (ILS): risk transformed into a tradeable asset, uncorrelated with stocks, that lets pension funds effectively underwrite hurricanes. The "rate-on-line" in this market is one of the truest real-time prices of global catastrophe risk.

③ The model-homogeneity fragility

A subtle, systemic danger. Under regimes like Solvency II, insurers must use certified risk models — and only a handful of vendors provide them. So much of the industry ends up pricing catastrophe risk through the same few models, which means they share the same blind spots. If those models are wrong in the same direction, the whole industry is mispriced simultaneously — a hidden, correlated fragility baked in by regulation meant to make the system safer.19 It's the 1693 problem in modern dress: everyone underwriting the same risk the same way.

④ Float as permanent capital — the PE play

Buffett's insight didn't stay his. Private-equity firms realised insurance float is the cheapest permanent capital in finance — money you control for years, paying near-zero to hold. The Apollo / Athene model (and KKR, Blackstone, Brookfield following) is built on acquiring or building life and annuity insurers, then investing the float into the PE firm's own higher-yielding credit strategies. It is Buffett's playbook, industrialised — and it's quietly reshaping who owns the world's retirement savings. When you see a PE giant buy an annuity provider, this is why: they're buying the float.

The unifying idea

Every advanced concept here — reinsurance, cat bonds, the PE roll-up — is ultimately a different answer to the same two questions the Lloyd's underwriters asked in 1688: how do we spread this risk so no single event ruins us, and how do we get paid to hold the money in between? Three centuries of financial engineering, and it still comes back to the float and the time-gap.

You now hold the thread the whole industry hangs on.

An insurer collects money now and pays it later. The gap is the float. Whether that float is a gift or a tax is decided by the combined ratio. Buffett's entire empire is the disciplined exploitation of negative-cost float, leveraged into equities in a way regulators let almost no one else attempt. India is that same model at the dawn of its growth curve; the US is its mature, saturated form. And every exotic instrument on top — reinsurance, cat bonds, the PE roll-up — is just a more sophisticated way of asking the two questions from the coffee house.
